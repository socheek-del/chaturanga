# Chu Shogi rules editions, variants, and current play

## Which editions and variants are documented, and which form is best supported?

### Takeaway

Use the Chu Shogi Renmei’s 2019 competition rules as the product’s named baseline: it is the current rulebook published by the Japanese governing association and is the best evidenced formal ruleset in current tournament use. The evidence supports “best documented/current association standard,” not a measured claim that this edition is the most popular.

### Cited Findings

- The Renmei’s rules page is explicitly titled “2019 edition” and says Chu Shogi is played on 12×12 with 46 pieces per side (92 total), no drops, 21 starting piece types, and 29 total types including promoted forms. ([Chu Shogi Renmei, 2019 rules, corrected 14 September 2019](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/kouza/rule2007.htm))
- The Renmei’s 2004 supplement says its earlier official rules had followed an interpretation of Okazaki’s rules, but it proposed adopting the rules of the historical *Chūshōgi Zenshū* and *Shinan-shō* sources instead, after comparison with other organizations. It records consequential lion-rule distinctions, including unrestricted lion capture when two lions are adjacent and different handling of first-lion and “tsukegui” captures. ([Chu Shogi Renmei, supplemental rule notes, 2004 revision](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/kouza/rule_hosoku2004.htm))
- The Renmei reproduces passages attributed to *Chūshōgi Shinan-shō*, dated Genroku 16 (1703), including its historical rules for lions and promotion. This is evidence of a documented historical source behind the current reconstruction, not evidence that every historical tournament followed one unchanged interpretation. ([Chu Shogi Renmei, historical source excerpts](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/kouza/Okazaki.htm))
- George Hodges’s *Middle Shogi Manual* was published in 1992 and revised in a second edition in 2002; it became a principal English-language reference. ([Abstract Games, issue 18, 2020, referencing the manual](https://www.abstractgames.org/uploads/1/1/6/4/116462923/AG18.cdr))
- Eric Silverman’s 5 April 2020 rules guide says it presents Renmei rules and notes that the rules often used in the West differ, particularly in promotion and king-baring. The guide recommends the Renmei rules on the basis of the historical text evidence it discusses. ([Dr Eric Silverman, 5 April 2020](https://drericsilverman.com/2020/04/05/chu-shogi-part-i-how-to-play/))
- Lishogi launched a digital Chu Shogi implementation on 26 December 2022 and maintains its own rule page. Its explanation states a four-rank promotion zone, no piece drops, and eligibility to promote upon entering the zone or capturing in/while leaving it. ([Lishogi launch announcement, 26 December 2022](https://lishogi.org/blog/post/Y6kTwxEAAKKgP_sV); [Lishogi Chu Shogi rules](https://lishogi.org/variant/chushogi))
- “Modern Chu Shogi” (*Gendai Chūshōgi*) is a separately described redesign, not merely a rules edition of the 12×12 game: its own site describes an 11×11 board, 80 pieces, and replacement rules such as piece exchange/sharpening. The page describes differences from classical Chu Shogi, including a changed Blind Tiger movement and no “first lion” rule. Evidence of organized play or broad adoption for this redesign was not found. ([Gendai Chu Shogi rules site, page history last updated 22 November 2010](https://seesaawiki.jp/nari_shogi/))

### Inferences

- For one interoperable, historically grounded implementation, identify the game specifically as “Chu Shogi — Chu Shogi Renmei 2019 rules.” This avoids treating the English Hodges interpretation, Lishogi’s digital rules, and the 11×11 modern redesign as identical without claiming they have equal use.
- The standard 12×12 setup appears common across the contemporary classic implementations reviewed; the significant disputes are about interpretation and endings rather than a second commonly played classical starting array.

### Gaps

- No population or tournament dataset compares Renmei, Hodges, Lishogi, and other rule interpretations. The “most popular edition” cannot be established from available evidence.
- The exact treatment of checkmate in the Renmei’s Japanese text needs a rules decision before implementation: its 2019 page lists checkmating the king or physically capturing it as winning, while Silverman’s English explanation says capture is required and checkmate alone is not enough. These accounts should not be silently merged. ([Renmei 2019 rules](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/kouza/rule2007.htm); [Silverman, 5 April 2020](https://drericsilverman.com/2020/04/05/chu-shogi-part-i-how-to-play/))

## What contemporary play evidence exists, and what does it show about popularity?

### Takeaway

Chu Shogi has documented contemporary tournament activity in Japan and online, but the available numbers show a small specialist community, not broad popularity or a reliable edition ranking. Its strongest comparative adoption signal is that it remains the only large-board historical Shogi variant reported as still being played by people in recent academic writing.

### Cited Findings

- A 2015 Game Programming Workshop paper reports that a few people in Japan and abroad still play Chu Shogi, while the other large Shogi variants discussed had no current players. The paper is useful comparative evidence within the large-Shogi family; it does not count players or distinguish rules editions. ([20th Game Programming Workshop, 2015 paper PDF](https://ipsj.ixsq.nii.ac.jp/records/145794/files/IPSJ-GPWS2015019.pdf))
- The Renmei’s tournament record lists a recurring national tournament from 2000 through its 25th edition in August 2026, and a separate Meijin series through its 22nd edition in January 2026. The same record lists the 24th national tournament in September 2025. ([Chu Shogi Renmei tournament records, accessed 26 November 2026](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/record.htm))
- The Renmei’s detailed report for its January 2022 national tournament records six A-class and four B-class entrants. Its January 2021 event combined classes due to COVID restrictions and listed eight players. ([Chu Shogi Renmei tournament reports](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/taikai/zenkoku/index.htm))
- Lishogi supports online human-versus-human Chu Shogi. Its launch post announced the game on 26 December 2022 and said computer play and analysis were not supported there at launch; it also warned that HaChu had rule differences that could create move-compatibility problems. ([Lishogi launch announcement, 26 December 2022](https://lishogi.org/blog/post/Y6kTwxEAAKKgP_sV))
- Lishogi’s Chu Shogi Club page showed 201 members when accessed on 26 November 2026. The page lists a 2023 Chu Shogi Cup and a third Classical Swiss tournament in 2025; the tournament forum reports a five-round event and names the top finishers. Membership is a platform group count, not a count of active players or games. ([Lishogi Chu Shogi Club](https://lishogi.org/team/chu-shogi-club); [2025 Classical Swiss registration post](https://lishogi.org/forum/general-shogi-discussion/chu-shogi-classical-swiss-3rd-edition-2025))
- The German Chu Shogi Association says it was founded in January 2006 to organize tournaments and publish annotated games. Its page describes the game as little known even among Japanese Shogi players; the page did not establish recent event frequency. ([German Chu Shogi Association, organization overview](https://www.chushogi.de/aboutus.htm))
- Silverman’s 2020 overview characterizes the Western following as small, notes online and correspondence play, and points readers to HaChu as a strong dedicated engine. ([Dr Eric Silverman, 5 April 2020](https://drericsilverman.com/2020/04/05/chu-shogi-part-i-how-to-play/))

### Inferences

- Tournament records and current server support establish that the classic game remains playable and organized. The small reported in-person fields and absence of user-count time series mean this is evidence of continuity, not growth or relative popularity.
- The Japanese Renmei rules have the strongest evidence of actual over-the-board tournament use. Lishogi has the clearest current evidence of an online casual player ecosystem, but its membership and tournament data do not establish that its exact rules interpretation is the predominant one.

### Gaps

- No reliable, comparable figures were found for total players, active accounts, number of games by rules edition, tournament participation across years, or downloads of Chu Shogi clients.
- Lishogi’s announcement documents missing computer play and analysis in December 2022, but a current live capability audit was outside this research task. HaChu’s existence alone does not establish easy integration or rules compatibility.

## Which rule differences should a digital product make explicit?

### Takeaway

The product should name its rules edition and explicitly teach lion trading, promotion eligibility, and royal-piece endings. These are the documented areas where current public rules differ or are described inconsistently.

### Cited Findings

- **Setup and capture:** the Renmei defines 12×12, 46 pieces per player and captured pieces removed rather than reused. This is also the base form described by Lishogi’s rules page. ([Renmei 2019 rules](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/kouza/rule2007.htm); [Lishogi rules](https://lishogi.org/variant/chushogi))
- **Lion rules:** Renmei’s 2019 rules allow direct capture when lions are adjacent; when they are one square apart, a protected lion cannot be captured directly by the other lion. Its rules also cover indirect support (“shadow”/“hidden” protection), a first-capture privilege after a non-lion captures a lion, and the exception allowing a lion to capture a lion together with another eligible piece (“tsukegui”); pawns and go-betweens do not qualify for that extra capture. ([Renmei 2019 lion rules](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/kouza/rule2007.htm))
- The Renmei says its earlier official interpretation was based on Okazaki, then changed in 2004 to readings of older rule texts. Historical Shinan-shō text reproduced by the Renmei describes the separated-lion protection, capture of an intervening piece together with the lion, immediate recapture of the capturing lion, and the “first lion” rule. This is a real edition history, not merely terminology variation. ([Renmei 2004 supplemental notes](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/kouza/rule_hosoku2004.htm); [Renmei historical text](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/kouza/Okazaki.htm))
- Lishogi summarizes lion capture with three conditions (adjacent, unprotected, and a significant extra capture), followed by a counterstrike restriction on a non-lion recapture. Its rule page also says a pawn/go-between used to expose a lion does not make that newly exposed lion capturable in the same move. ([Lishogi rules](https://lishogi.org/variant/chushogi))
- **Promotion:** Renmei rules make promotion optional when a piece enters the enemy’s four-rank zone from outside; after declining, a piece gets another opportunity by leaving and re-entering, or by capturing in the zone or while leaving it. A pawn gets an additional chance on reaching the last rank; a non-promoted pawn or lance on its last rank is immobile. For correspondence games only, the rules page extends the pawn exception to lances. ([Renmei 2019 promotion rules](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/kouza/rule2007.htm))
- Lishogi says pawns and lances both get a second promotion chance on the last rank. This differs from the Renmei’s over-the-board formulation, where the special second chance is stated for the pawn and the lance allowance is marked for correspondence games. ([Lishogi rules](https://lishogi.org/variant/chushogi); [Renmei 2019 rules](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/kouza/rule2007.htm))
- HaChu’s maintainer documents a further Western promotion interpretation: after declining a promotion, Hodges’s manual and HaChu’s alternative setting disagree about whether the same piece may promote on a later non-capturing move in the zone. ([H.G. Muller, *Middle Shogi Manual* errata B](https://hgm.nubati.net/MSM/ChuMatesB.html))
- **Endings:** the Renmei’s 2019 text says the opponent’s king must be checkmated or captured and, if a prince is present, that must also be defeated. It also states a material “bare” ending and mutual-consent draw provision; repetition rules require the player responsible for the repeated cycle to change before the fourth occurrence. ([Renmei 2019 rules](https://www.chushogi-renmei.com/kouza/rule2007.htm))
- Silverman describes the Renmei form as requiring capture rather than checkmate, and describes a bare-king rule under which leaving only a king/prince wins unless the opponent can bare the player in reply (draw) or capture the player’s king (loss). ([Silverman, 5 April 2020](https://drericsilverman.com/2020/04/05/chu-shogi-part-i-how-to-play/))
- Lishogi’s own rules page also defines a bare-king outcome and fourfold repetition with a loss for the side continuing perpetual capture threats. ([Lishogi rules](https://lishogi.org/variant/chushogi))

### Inferences

- The core 12×12 rules can be kept stable while treating Renmei 2019 as the default and documenting the Lishogi/Hodges differences as compatibility notes. If interoperability with Lishogi is a target, promotion and endings should be compared move-by-move against the live server rather than assumed identical from the shared board and piece set.
- A tutorial should teach lion protection and the counterstrike/tsukegui exceptions with examples, because the leading online rules page itself warns that these rules are complex. It should also distinguish “promotion is optional” from “promotion opportunity is conditional.”

### Gaps

- The Renmei’s Japanese wording for checkmate versus physical king capture is not consistent with Silverman’s English explanation. Resolve this against a full Japanese translation or tournament practice before implementing check detection and victory.
- Public material reviewed does not settle every edge case in the lion rules across the 2004 Okazaki interpretation, Hodges’s manual, HaChu, and Lishogi; engine-level conformance should target one declared edition rather than silently blending them.
