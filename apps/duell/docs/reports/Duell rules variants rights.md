# Trace Duell’s rules and rights ownership

The roadmap’s Duell is the **1976 Lakeside edition of Geoffrey Hayes’s dice-as-pieces game**, part of the earlier *Conquest/Duell* rules family. The clearest surviving material describes a 9×8 board, eight numbered dice and one King die per side, tumbling orthogonal moves, exact-landing captures, and a King/key-square victory. Later 9×9 *Tactix* is a separate revision, and the evidence does not establish either edition as the most popular. The 1976 materials also leave the opening-player convention and key-square win timing in conflict. **No digital-adaptation permission or applicable license was found, and present-day ownership remains unresolved, so repository policy blocks implementation pending rights clearance.** ([1976 rules scan](https://regle.escaleajeux.fr/duel__rg.pdf), [US Patent 3,929,337](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3929337A/en), [Duell editions and adoption evidence](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgame/2409/duell))

## Hayes’s 1976 Lakeside game belongs to the Conquest family

The roadmap’s “standard 1976 edition” points to Lakeside’s U.S. *Duell*. Geoffrey Hayes’s design appeared earlier in the UK as *Conquest* under Denys Fisher Games; a contemporaneous 1976 review identified Hayes as inventor and later names including *Duell*. The patent names Hayes as inventor and Toy Development Ltd as assignee, while surviving product records identify Lakeside as the U.S. publisher. These records establish the game’s lineage and edition more clearly than they establish current ownership. ([1976 Games & Puzzles review](https://archive.org/details/sim_games-and-puzzles_1976-05_48), [Parlett’s Conquest history](https://www.parlettgames.uk/gamester/conquest.html), [US Patent 3,929,337](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3929337A/en), [Lakeside Duell catalog record](https://www.nobleknight.com/P/2147993286/Duell))

The rules family moved across publishers and names, but the later Schmidt Spiele *Tactix* changed the board to 9×9 and reportedly lets the King capture, unlike classic 8×9 *Duell*. This makes *Tactix* a distinct variant rather than a substitute for the roadmap’s Lakeside edition. Reissues and a 306-rating BoardGameGeek page show circulation and hobbyist activity, while a specialist review describes a persistent following; neither supplies comparative sales, player counts, or tournament data. The defensible selection is therefore **the best-documented roadmap edition, not a popularity winner**. ([LUDING’s Duell/Tactix review](https://www.luding.org/reviews/bpk/Tactix.html), [BoardGameGeek Duell ratings](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgame/2409/duell/ratings))

## Nine dice turn board movement into orientation state

Each side fields **eight ordinary six-sided dice and one King die**, on a 9×8 board. The surviving 1976 rules scan shows a back rank ordered 5, 1, 2, 6, King, 6, 2, 1, 5; ordinary dice show their 3 face toward their player. A numbered die moves exactly the number showing before it starts. It rolls orthogonally, may turn through one right angle, cannot pass another die, and captures only by landing on an opposing die. Each roll changes the die’s orientation, so a move’s route determines its next top face and future movement range. The King advances one square. A player wins by capturing the opposing King or reaching the square where that King began. ([1976 rules scan](https://regle.escaleajeux.fr/duel__rg.pdf), [US Patent 3,929,337](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3929337A/en), [1976 Conquest review](https://s3.amazonaws.com/arena-attachments/924462/5a983325d935ae0c8e1cba17ece53837.pdf))

The patent’s movement examples confirm that rolling direction changes which face rises to the top, and that two equal-length routes can leave a die with different top values. A future rules engine would therefore need to preserve the full face orientation, not just the current pip count. However, the scanned setup does not expose a complete standard-die chirality diagram in its text layer; the starting orientations should be verified against the original illustrated leaflet before writing fixtures. ([US Patent 3,929,337](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3929337A/en), [1976 rules scan](https://regle.escaleajeux.fr/duel__rg.pdf))

## Opening and victory timing remain unresolved

The 1976 French rules scan says White begins, while a Parker rules transcription says Red begins; a 2010 hobbyist transcription reports that the Lakeside English leaflet let players choose. Because the original Lakeside leaflet was not independently inspected, that convention cannot yet be assigned confidently to the selected edition. Victory timing also remains open: the printed objective says a King can reach the opponent’s starting square, but a contemporaneous review says the rules do not make clear whether this wins immediately or allows a reply. The review’s suggested “survive the reply” condition is an author-proposed refinement, not a verified original rule. ([1976 rules scan](https://regle.escaleajeux.fr/duel__rg.pdf), [SourceForge Duell transcription](https://sourceforge.net/projects/duell/files/), [1976 review](https://s3.amazonaws.com/arena-attachments/924462/5a983325d935ae0c8e1cba17ece53837.pdf))

The sources do not specify what happens on no legal move, repetition, or a prolonged game. Before any implementation, verify the Lakeside opening clause and dice diagram, choose a sourced or explicitly labeled digital convention for key-square timing, and document any draw or no-move convention in the rules contract and tutorial. ([1976 rules scan](https://regle.escaleajeux.fr/duel__rg.pdf), [1976 review](https://s3.amazonaws.com/arena-attachments/924462/5a983325d935ae0c8e1cba17ece53837.pdf))

## Public rules scans do not establish adaptation rights

The 1976 U.S. edition was published by Lakeside, while the patent record identifies Geoffrey Hayes and Toy Development Ltd. Google Patents marks the patent expired but cautions that its status is an assumption, not a legal conclusion. No chain of title was found for the game, product name, or artwork. Hasbro’s corporate history documents its acquisition of Parker Brothers, but no evidence ties Lakeside or the Duell rights to Hasbro; Hasbro’s digital-content licensing policy applies only if Hasbro confirms it controls the relevant rights. ([US Patent 3,929,337](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3929337A/en), [Hasbro corporate timeline](https://cdn.hasbro.com/faaa3d5019832273570327fcdc971786a399f134/3f48867d684706bf14abe585a5714b83.pdf), [Hasbro Digital Content Guidelines](https://docs.hasbro.com/en-us/legal/DigitalContentGuidelines))

The U.S. Copyright Office says game ideas and methods of play are not protected by copyright, while expressive rules text and board artwork can be. That general guidance does not settle trademark, contract, patent, ownership, or non-U.S. questions, and it is not a project license. No digital license or written adaptation permission was located. Under the repository’s rights gate, implementation must wait until the relevant rights holder is identified and written permission covering a free GPL-3.0 digital adaptation—or an applicable license—is documented. If cleared, the product should still use independently written rules and lessons, original artwork, and separate name clearance before public branding. ([U.S. Copyright Office: Games](https://www.copyright.gov/register/tx-games.html), [Circular 22: Investigating Copyright Status](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ22.pdf), [Copyright Public Records Portal](https://www.copyright.gov/public-records/))

## Conclusion

The rules question is tractable once the Lakeside leaflet and illustrated die setup are verified; the rights question is not. A patent’s apparent expiry and the availability of scanned rules do not identify the current owner or authorize a digital adaptation. **Keep Duell blocked before implementation until both rights clearance and the edition-level rules gaps are resolved.**
