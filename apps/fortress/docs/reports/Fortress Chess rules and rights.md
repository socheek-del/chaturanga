## Petrov’s account identifies the Russian four-player game

The Wikipedia entry “Fortress chess (Russia)” points to Russian Fortress Chess, a four-player game associated with Russia in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Petrov’s “Das Vierschach mit Festungen” (1850) and his nearly identical 1862 Russian description are the strongest rules sources: Markov calls them the only detailed, reliable firsthand descriptions, while an 1853 chess manual independently directs readers to Petrov for the rules. This distinguishes the target from generic European four-handed chess and from later two-player fortress adaptations. ([Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_abstract_strategy_games#Chess_and_chess-like_games), [Fortress chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortress_chess), [Markov 2015](https://bgsj.ludus-opuscula.org/PDF_Files/41_49_Markov_web.pdf), [1853 *Lehrbuch des Schachspiels*](https://www.textgridrep.org/browse/4bj4w.0))

No popularity ranking can be defended for this entry. Markov cites Petrov’s letter describing the game as not particularly popular and naming only a few St. Petersburg players; a 2023 overview later calls it popular but gives no participation, club, or tournament measures. The London Four-Handed Chess Club documented from 1885 played a different general four-player game, not Russian Fortress Chess. Since no comparative popularity data surfaced, the plan’s “most popular” rule cannot select an edition here; use Petrov because his rules are the strongest historical record. ([Markov 2015](https://bgsj.ludus-opuscula.org/PDF_Files/41_49_Markov_web.pdf), [2023 overview](https://inion.ru/site/assets/files/7614/2023_e_bol_shaia_evraziia_6_1.pdf))

## The 192-cell board and team objective are the stable core

The game has **192 playable squares**: a 128-square four-handed central board plus four 4×4 corner fortresses. Four people play as two opposite-seat teams, taking clockwise turns. Each player has the ordinary chess army plus an extra rook, bishop, and knight in the fortress on that player’s right. Pieces mostly use chess movement, but fortress walls block routes; the knight’s move consists of one orthogonal and one diagonal step in either order rather than a wall-crossing jump. Markov gives concrete gate lines and examples for forbidden diagonal routes at wall ends; Ludii supplies an independently usable board description and confirms that two wall segments flank each fortress. The 16×16 coordinate envelope uses the Wiki diagram's 192-square footprint: the c–n / ranks 3–14 central rectangle plus the four a–d/m–p corner 4×4s, with overlaps counted once. Pawns move forward, capture diagonally, may advance two squares initially, and allied pieces do not capture one another. ([Markov 2015](https://bgsj.ludus-opuscula.org/PDF_Files/41_49_Markov_web.pdf), [Ludii Portal](https://ludii.games/details.php?keyword=Russian+Fortress+Chess))

A team wins by checkmating both opposing players. A checkmated player is eliminated and that player’s pieces are removed immediately; leaving them frozen on the board belongs to another four-handed tradition. Pawns can promote on an ally’s or opponent’s ordinary home rank. Markov treats fortress promotion as unclear and recommends the literal reading that limits promotion to ordinary home ranks. The shape and walls are described in enough detail to implement the mapped footprint and test move rays without copying the historical diagram. ([Markov 2015](https://bgsj.ludus-opuscula.org/PDF_Files/41_49_Markov_web.pdf), [Ludii Portal](https://ludii.games/details.php?keyword=Russian+Fortress+Chess))

## Setup and endings need a primary-text check

The most important remaining conflict is how players place the three extra fortress pieces. Markov's figure caption says they are randomly placed; Ludii and later summaries say players may place them freely. Use an interactive placement phase so each human can arrange them and bots can choose a legal setup. Markov and Ludii settle gates/walls sufficiently for the digital model; the fixed seat order will be top red, right blue, bottom white, left black, with Red+White versus Blue+Black. Markov confirms castling exists but does not spell out its full restrictions; the model will use orthodox castling checks along each player's axis, with wall crossings forbidden. En passant will follow the orthodox immediate-reply rule against an enemy pawn. Promotion inside a fortress is unclear, so promote only on the four ordinary home ranks. Stalemate is unknown; follow Markov's preferred immediate-draw interpretation whenever an active seat has no legal move without being in check. These last decisions are digital reconstruction choices, not claims about Petrov's exact text. ([Markov 2015](https://bgsj.ludus-opuscula.org/PDF_Files/41_49_Markov_web.pdf), [Ludii Portal](https://ludii.games/details.php?keyword=Russian+Fortress+Chess), [German summary](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russisches_Festungsschach))
