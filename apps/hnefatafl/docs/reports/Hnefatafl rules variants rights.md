# Copenhagen rules add shieldwalls and forts

Choose **Copenhagen Hnefatafl on an 11×11 board** as the documented modern tournament ruleset for this product. Its named contributors introduced shieldwall capture, encirclement, an exit-fort win and a loss for the defender on perpetual repetition, distinguishing it from Fetlar and historical reconstructions. The evidence supports calling Copenhagen a tournament standard, but it does not establish that it is the most popular Hnefatafl variant worldwide. The rules are asymmetric: 24 attackers try to capture or surround a king and 12 defenders, while the defenders try to reach a corner or establish an unbreakable exit fort. The detailed rules and rights research also uncover implementation risks: the starting array must be transcribed from the official diagram, exit-fort adjudication is not machine-defined, and neither a digital-use license nor the present rights arrangement was found. ([Copenhagen rules](https://aagenielsen.dk/copenhagen_rules.php), [World Tafl Federation Championship 2026](https://aagenielsen.dk/wtfturnering2026.php), [Copenhagen rules PDF](https://aagenielsen.dk/Copenhagen_Hnefatafl_11x11.pdf))

## Copenhagen is the documented tournament choice

Copenhagen was outlined in 2012 by Aage Nielsen, Adam Bartley and Tim Millar as a modern development of Fetlar; the official English rules credit Bartley for the text and diagrams. Nielsen's rules index calls Copenhagen the modern tournament variant and labels Fetlar the classic modern variant. The World Tafl Federation's 2026 championship page continues to run a distinct Copenhagen 11×11 line alongside historical tafl. Together these sources establish an active, named tournament format—not a measured global ranking of variants. Historical weak-king games and Fetlar remain distinct, so their starting arrays, king-capture rules and draw outcomes should not silently enter this product. ([Copenhagen rules](https://aagenielsen.dk/copenhagen_rules.php), [rules index](https://aagenielsen.dk/tafl_rules.php), [World Tafl Federation Championship 2026](https://aagenielsen.dk/wtfturnering2026.php), [Fetlar rules](https://aagenielsen.dk/fetlar_rules_en.php))

## The 11×11 game has asymmetric goals and turns

The official diagram shows 24 attackers, 12 ordinary defenders and a king on the central throne; attackers move first, and the two sides alternate. All pieces slide any number of vacant squares orthogonally. Only the king may occupy the four corners or central throne; empty restricted squares can assist captures. Because the rule text points to an image instead of listing coordinates, the array needs a cell-by-cell transcription and an independent symmetry check before a setup fixture can be trusted. ([Copenhagen rules](https://aagenielsen.dk/copenhagen_rules.php), [Copenhagen rules PDF](https://aagenielsen.dk/Copenhagen_Hnefatafl_11x11.pdf))

Ordinary pieces are captured when a move closes a two-sided sandwich against an enemy or a hostile restricted square; simply moving between existing enemies does not capture. The king has a separate surround rule: attackers capture it on four sides, or on three sides next to the throne, but not while it stands at the board edge. Defenders win when the king reaches a corner or qualifies for an exit fort at the edge. Attackers can also win by surrounding the king and all remaining defenders. These are separate outcome checks and need their own exercises in both roles. ([Copenhagen rules](https://aagenielsen.dk/copenhagen_rules.php))

## Shieldwalls and exit forts need explicit adjudication

Copenhagen shieldwall capture removes a contiguous line of at least two enemy pieces at the board edge when a flanking move brackets the line and each enclosed piece has an enemy directly in front; a corner can replace an endpoint. The king can help form or close the wall but survives inside a captured wall. Other summaries omit the requirement that the capture follow a flanking move, so the product should follow the detailed official PDF wording and test corner substitutions and king cases against its diagrams. ([Copenhagen rules PDF, §3.1](https://aagenielsen.dk/Copenhagen_Hnefatafl_11x11.pdf), [Taflhús rule explanation](https://taflhus.com/learn/rules))

An exit fort is not merely a king touching the edge: the king must have a legal move and the attackers must be unable to break the formation. The source explains the result with examples but does not provide a computational test. The engine contract will need either a complete reachability test consistent with those examples or a narrower, clearly documented convention backed by a rules source; a static “king is on edge” check would change the game. ([Copenhagen rules](https://aagenielsen.dk/copenhagen_rules.php), [Copenhagen rules PDF, §5.1](https://aagenielsen.dk/Copenhagen_Hnefatafl_11x11.pdf))

## Repetition and ending rules still need a digital contract

A player with no legal move loses. If the game cannot be ended—for example, both sides lack enough material—the rules allow a draw. Perpetual repetition is forbidden and the source assigns the loss to White, the defender. It does not define whether any third occurrence loses, how to identify a perpetual cycle, or which positions qualify as impossible to finish. The engine should not import orthodox chess's threefold or material rules without a Copenhagen source. ([Copenhagen rules](https://aagenielsen.dk/copenhagen_rules.php), [Copenhagen summary](https://aagenielsen.dk/copenhagen_summary.php))

## No Copenhagen digital-use permission was found

The selected rules are a modern authored ruleset, not simply an ancient game recovered from history. The official page asserts copyright and credits three contributors; it credits Bartley with the English text and diagrams. No contributor agreement, rights assignment, current licensing contact or digital-adaptation permission was located. U.S. and U.K. copyright guidance distinguishes procedures from expressive rules text and art, so independent code and original content may have a legal basis in some places, but that does not supply global clearance or meet this repository's stricter requirement for a permission or applicable license covering a faithful digital project. A separate AGPL-licensed Hnefatafl engine licenses its code only; it does not clear the Copenhagen authors' text, diagrams or branding. No rights holder was contacted. ([Copenhagen rules](https://aagenielsen.dk/copenhagen_rules.php), [U.S. Copyright Office: Games](https://www.copyright.gov/register/tx-games.html), [UK IPO: Copyright](https://www.gov.uk/copyright/overview), [AGPL engine source](https://docs.rs/hnefatafl-copenhagen/latest/src/hnefatafl_copenhagen/lib.rs.html))

## Conclusion

Copenhagen gives the product a strong, testable identity: asymmetric sides, edge-sensitive captures and a defender escape rule that goes beyond a simple king race. Its source gaps are tractable only after the licensing gate clears; then the first rules task is to transcribe the official array and formalize the exit-fort and repetition tests against the published examples.

Under the repository's `AGENTS.md`, keep implementation blocked until written permission from the relevant rights holders or an applicable license covers the intended adaptation. Keep product-name clearance separate, and write original rules explanations, tutorial prose and board artwork even if permission is obtained.
