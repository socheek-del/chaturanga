# Choose a Documented Jeson Mor Edition

Implement the **9×9 checkerboard form of Jeson Mor**: two players each start with nine horse-shaped chess knights, move them by ordinary knight jumps, and win by either capturing every opposing piece or taking a knight to e5 and later moving it away. The sources describe one traditional game, not multiple editions with a measured popularity ranking, so the 9×9 form is the best-supported default rather than a provably most popular variant. Assia Popova’s ethnographic account supports the board, pieces, knight movement and central race; the later Pritchard rules, as cited by Wikipedia, supply the explicit first-rank setup and reach-then-leave win. Keep that later win condition distinct from Fairy-Stockfish’s digital shortcut, which awards victory once a knight survives the opponent’s reply. The project should write its own rules, code, tutorial, artwork and branding: the consulted sources do not grant reuse rights or establish worldwide clearance.

## The 9×9 board is evidenced; popularity is not ranked

Popova’s 1974 study reports Mongolian games observed in 1963–1966 and discusses Jesön Mor on a 9×9 board with nine horse pieces per player. Ludii’s evidence record quotes the relevant passage and attributes it to Popova’s pages 32–35 and figure VI.2; the record says the horses move like chess knights, can capture, and race toward the central e5 square. The underlying Mongolian source is identified as *Mongolyn Togloom*, but its full text was not independently inspected ([Persée article](https://www.persee.fr/doc/emong_0150-3014_1974_num_5_1_909), [Ludii ethnographic record](https://ludii.games/data.php?keyword=Mongolia)).

Wikipedia’s account, citing David Pritchard’s 1994 and 2007 chess-variant encyclopedias, describes nine knights on each player’s first rank and lets players choose who moves first. It also mentions an 8×8 grid played on intersections. That sounds like an alternate physical drawing of the same nine-by-nine playable points, but the inspected source does not fully specify that equipment; treating it as a different rules edition would be unsupported. Neither the cited sources nor the other consulted catalogs provide adoption, tournament, sales or survey data that would establish which representation is most popular ([Wikipedia’s rules and references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeson_Mor), [BoardGameGeek rules wiki](https://boardgamegeek.com/wiki/page/thing%3A37917)).

## Nine knights race from home ranks to e5

For a digital board, use files a–i and ranks 1–9, place one army on rank 1 and the other on rank 9, and mark e5 as the center. The ethnographic record supports the 9×9 field and nine horse-shaped movers per side; the first-rank setup and choice of first player come from the later Pritchard summary cited by Wikipedia. All pieces move as chess knights: an L-shaped jump to a destination two squares along one axis and one along the other, with intervening pieces ignored. A knight may land on an empty square or capture an opponent’s knight, including one on e5 ([Ludii](https://ludii.games/data.php?keyword=Mongolia), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeson_Mor), [Ludii’s playable Jesön Mor entry](https://ludii.games/details.php?keyword=Jeson+Mor)).

This edition has no kings, check, castling or promotion in the consulted descriptions. Popova’s account characterizes capture as secondary to reaching the center; the later Pritchard/Wikipedia account makes capturing every opposing piece an alternative victory. Repetition, move-count draws and stalemate handling appear in digital engine implementations rather than in the historical evidence, so any such endings should be stated as product conventions rather than attributed to the traditional rules ([Ludii](https://ludii.games/data.php?keyword=Mongolia), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeson_Mor), [Fairy-Stockfish’s variant documentation](https://fairy-stockfish.github.io/variants/)).

## Reaching e5 starts a contest, not an instant win

The strongest playable wording is the Pritchard/Wikipedia rule: a player wins by moving a knight to e5 and then moving it off e5 on a later move; the opponent can capture the knight while it sits there. BoardGameGeek’s rules summary agrees with this interpretation. Popova’s excerpt supports a race to e5 but does not describe the escape sequence, so the reach-then-leave requirement belongs to the later codification, not to the accessible ethnographic passage ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeson_Mor), [BoardGameGeek](https://boardgamegeek.com/wiki/page/thing%3A37917), [Ludii’s excerpt of Popova](https://ludii.games/data.php?keyword=Mongolia)).

One timing detail remains open: the later wording does not clearly say whether the same knight must leave on its owner’s immediately following turn or may wait while other pieces move. For a deterministic digital implementation, require the **same knight** that entered e5 to leave it on its next turn, after the opponent has had one reply and a chance to capture it. This is an explicit product clarification that preserves the reach-then-leave condition. Do not silently substitute Fairy-Stockfish’s `flagMove` behavior, which wins after the e5 knight survives only the opponent’s reply, without requiring it to leave. Its threefold-repetition, 50-move and stalemate-loss settings should likewise remain separate from the historical rule contract ([PyChess’s generated Jeson Mor definition](https://www.pychess.org/variants/jesonmor), [Fairy-Stockfish variants](https://fairy-stockfish.github.io/variants/)).

## Original content is the practical rights boundary

The U.S. Copyright Office says game ideas, titles and methods of play are not protected by U.S. copyright, while written rules and graphic art can be protected expression. Mongolia’s 2021 copyright-law translation excludes folklore and national traditions and ideas or methods from that law, but also lists derivative folklore works as protected and directs folk-art protection to other laws; WIPO notes that traditional cultural expressions can intersect with several legal systems. These sources support implementing factual game procedures independently, but they do not establish worldwide permission for this particular traditional game ([U.S. Copyright Office: Games](https://www.copyright.gov/register/tx-games.html), [WIPO Lex: Mongolia’s copyright law](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/legislation/details/8306), [WIPO on traditional cultural expressions](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/traditional-knowledge/traditional-cultural-expressions/index)).

The inspected Pritchard references, VariChess page and itch.io prototype did not state an open license for their prose, artwork or code. Write a fresh rules contract and tutorial, draw original horse pieces and board art, and create a distinct visual identity; do not copy encyclopedia text, Wikimedia diagrams or another implementation’s assets without checking the exact item’s license. No Mongolian-law opinion, trademark search or jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction clearance was completed, so the report is an implementation boundary, not a global legal clearance ([Wikipedia bibliography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeson_Mor), [VariChess](https://varichess.org/play/jesonmor), [itch.io prototype](https://hidroto.itch.io/jeson-mor), [Wikimedia content reuse](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Wikimedia_APIs/Content_reuse)).

## Conclusion

The rules engine should represent the center goal as a pending, capturable objective tied to the knight that entered e5; a surviving reply alone is not victory in the selected edition. Tests should cover capture on e5, surviving the reply, leaving e5 with the same knight, and capturing all enemy pieces. This keeps the product faithful to the later published reach-then-leave account while openly documenting the timing convention that the historical evidence leaves unstated.
