# Kruzno identity, variants, and adoption

## What does “Kruzno” refer to in the catalog?

### Takeaway
The Wikipedia entry is Mark Palko’s commercial 2005 abstract game, usually described with three chess-piece types and intransitive captures. Kruzno’s own site also publishes a separate “Kruzno Checkers” ruleset for the same board; that variant has a different setup and ordinary checkers-style jumps. The roadmap wording about the “linked hex-checkers game” may mean this separate ruleset, so the product plan must say whether it is digitizing the base game, Kruzno Checkers, or both.

### Cited Findings
- The Kruzno (board game) Wikipedia entry says it is a two-player abstract game first published in 2005 and describes its “most common variant” as three rooks, knights, and bishops per player, with intransitive captures, non-capturing jumps, and an outer-ring restriction. [Wikipedia: Kruzno (board game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kruzno_(board_game))
- That article is explicitly tagged with a notability concern and its sole printed-book citation is David Parlett’s *Oxford History of Board Games* (1999), which predates the article’s claimed 2005 publication. The citation therefore cannot, without further explanation, substantiate the 2005 game’s own rules or publication date. [Wikipedia: Kruzno (board game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kruzno_(board_game))
- The official Kruzno site describes “Kruzno Checkers” separately as a variant designed for a Kruzno board. It starts with twelve pieces, has no promotion, allows a one-hex adjacent move, restricts the shaded border, requires a capture when available, and lets the player choose among capture options. [Kruzno.com: Hexagonal Checkers](https://www.kruzno.com/hexcheckers.html)
- The official store describes Kruzno as a board/game system for several games, explicitly listing Kruzno, hexagonal chess, hexagonal checkers, Hex, Agon, and other variants. This supports treating “Kruzno Checkers” as a separate rules option on the Kruzno board rather than silently substituting it for the base game. [Kruzno.com: Game Store](https://www.kruzno.com/stored.html)
- A regional tourism/community page attributes Kruzno to Mark Palko, says he first published it in 2005, and reports that the village of Kružno contacted him and purchased three sets in 2011. [Región Malohont: Hra Kružno](https://regionmalohont.sk/volny-cas/hra-kruzno-outdorova-aktivita-kruzno/)

### Inferences
- The catalog and official-site materials refer to two related but distinct playable rule sets: the chess-piece base game and the American-checkers-derived Kruzno Checkers variant. This follows from the separate names and materially different setups/rules on the [Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kruzno_(board_game)) and [official Kruzno Checkers page](https://www.kruzno.com/hexcheckers.html).
- The roadmap phrase “linked hex-checkers game” likely points to Kruzno Checkers, but it is ambiguous because the Wikipedia entry itself is about the 2005 base game and links to the general official site. The product contract should explicitly resolve this before rules work.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable independent source explaining why the Wikipedia gameplay summary cites a 1999 book for a game said to be published in 2005.
- The official site’s current page is sparse and relies partly on diagrams; its full historical publication and variant history were not independently documented.

## Which edition or variant is best supported?

### Takeaway
If the catalog means the base game, the clearest currently indexed candidate is the Wikipedia-described two-player game with nine pieces per player, while reduced-piece and three-player expansions are only reported by a secondary game database. If the catalog means Kruzno Checkers, the official 12-piece setup and jump rules are directly documented by the designer’s site. Neither source trail establishes comparative popularity.

### Cited Findings
- Wikipedia labels the nine-piece arrangement “the most common variant,” but provides no evidence or source for the comparative claim and carries a notability warning. Treat this as a weakly supported description, not a demonstrated popularity ranking. [Wikipedia: Kruzno (board game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kruzno_(board_game))
- The official Kruzno Checkers rules state an opening of twelve pieces, one-step adjacent moves when not capturing, no promotion, a restricted perimeter, mandatory captures, and freedom to choose among multiple captures. [Kruzno.com: Hexagonal Checkers](https://www.kruzno.com/hexcheckers.html)
- A Hungarian board-game catalogue lists Kruzno as a 2004 Mark Palko design for two or three players and reports reduced-piece versions plus three-player expansion sets. It is a secondary database entry, not a primary rule source. [Társasjátékok: Kruzno](https://tarsasjatekok.com/tarsasjatek/kruzno-2004)
- The official store presents Kruzno alongside other games playable on the Kruzno board, including hexagonal checkers. [Kruzno.com: Game Store](https://www.kruzno.com/stored.html)

### Inferences
- The official page is the best available rules source for Kruzno Checkers; its exact rules can be transcribed without importing the base game’s non-transitive capture cycle. [Kruzno.com: Hexagonal Checkers](https://www.kruzno.com/hexcheckers.html)
- If a single Kruzno product is required, the original base game is the primary catalog interpretation; Kruzno Checkers should be a separate named variant only if scope allows. This is an interpretation of the separate Wikipedia and official-site titles, not an independently documented owner decision.

### Gaps
- No source provides sales, player counts, tournament participation, or representative comparative adoption for the base game versus Kruzno Checkers.
- I found no primary documentation for the reduced-piece or three-player expansion set reported by Társasjátékok; its exact setup/rules remain unverified.

## What adoption evidence exists?

### Takeaway
Available evidence demonstrates publication, a small local public installation, and limited retail/catalog exposure. It does not support calling one Kruzno variant the most popular or estimating a global player base.

### Cited Findings
- The regional Malohont page reports that the village bought three sets in 2011 and installed a public outdoor Kruzno board in 2013, available for public play. This is concrete but local adoption evidence. [Región Malohont: Hra Kružno](https://regionmalohont.sk/volny-cas/hra-kruzno-outdorova-aktivita-kruzno/)
- Mark Palko’s 2014 blog describes him as Kruzno’s developer and says he was selling the game through Amazon; this is evidence of a small direct-sale effort, not of sales volume. [Mark Palko, “A quick note on Kružno”](https://observationalepidemiology.blogspot.com/2014/12/a-quick-note-on-kruzno-official-game-of.html)
- The official Kruzno store page offers a Kruzno set and lists several games for the board, but supplies no sales or player totals. [Kruzno.com: Game Store](https://www.kruzno.com/stored.html)
- Wikipedia’s Kruzno article has a notability warning, further indicating that its secondary-source coverage is limited. [Wikipedia: Kruzno (board game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kruzno_(board_game))

### Inferences
- Kruzno has a documented niche following and a specific local public installation; neither fact establishes a popularity ranking. [Región Malohont](https://regionmalohont.sk/volny-cas/hra-kruzno-outdorova-aktivita-kruzno/); [Palko’s 2014 blog](https://observationalepidemiology.blogspot.com/2014/12/a-quick-note-on-kruzno-official-game-of.html)

### Gaps
- No independent sales data, tournament records, reliable online-play counts, or representative player survey surfaced for either rule set.
